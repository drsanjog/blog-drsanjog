## Dysmenorrhoea (Painful Periods): Causes, Types and When It Is Not Normal

Dysmenorrhoea is the medical term for painful menstruation — cramping pelvic pain that occurs before or during a period and ranges from mild discomfort to pain severe enough to disrupt daily life. It is one of the most common gynaecological complaints among women of reproductive age, yet it remains significantly undertreated because pain during menstruation is often normalised or dismissed.

## What Is Dysmenorrhoea and How Common Is It?

Dysmenorrhoea is classified into two distinct types: **primary dysmenorrhoea**, in which no underlying pelvic pathology is identified, and **secondary dysmenorrhoea**, in which pain results from a diagnosable gynaecological condition.

Globally, dysmenorrhoea affects between 45% and 95% of women of reproductive age, making it the leading cause of short-term school and work absenteeism among young women [1]. In India, studies suggest that a substantial proportion of adolescent girls and young women experience moderate to severe dysmenorrhoea, with many not seeking medical attention because they believe the pain is normal.

The distinction between the two types matters enormously. Primary dysmenorrhoea is manageable and does not damage the reproductive organs. Secondary dysmenorrhoea — particularly when caused by endometriosis — can progress silently, affect fertility, and require targeted medical or surgical management if not addressed early.

## Primary Dysmenorrhoea: What Causes It?

Primary dysmenorrhoea is caused by the release of **prostaglandins** — lipid compounds produced by the uterine lining (endometrium) as it sheds during menstruation. Prostaglandins trigger uterine muscle contractions, which reduce blood flow to the uterus and generate ischaemic pain similar to muscle cramps.

### Key features of primary dysmenorrhoea

- Onset: typically within 6–24 months of menarche (first period), once ovulatory cycles are established
- Location: lower abdomen, often radiating to the lower back or upper thighs
- Timing: begins a few hours before or at the start of bleeding; usually resolves within 48–72 hours
- Association: no pelvic abnormality found on examination or ultrasound
- Pattern: tends to remain stable or improve slightly over time, and often improves after a first full-term pregnancy

Risk factors for more severe primary dysmenorrhoea include early menarche, longer or heavier periods, nulliparity (never having given birth), smoking, and a positive family history of painful periods.

## Secondary Dysmenorrhoea: Underlying Causes

Secondary dysmenorrhoea arises when an identifiable pelvic condition is driving the pain. The key clinical clue is that pain in secondary dysmenorrhoea tends to **worsen progressively over time** and may no longer be confined to the days of menstruation.

### Common causes

| Condition | How it causes pain | Additional symptoms |
|---|---|---|
| **Endometriosis** | Endometrial-like tissue outside the uterus bleeds cyclically, causing inflammation and scarring | Deep pelvic pain, pain during intercourse, pain on defaecation, subfertility |
| **Adenomyosis** | Endometrial tissue within the myometrium causes the uterus to enlarge and become tender | Heavy periods, bulky tender uterus on examination |
| **Uterine fibroids** | Submucosal fibroids distort the uterine cavity and increase prostaglandin production | Heavy bleeding, pelvic pressure, possible urinary symptoms |
| **Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)** | Ascending infection causes chronic pelvic inflammation and adhesions | Intermenstrual discharge, fever (in acute PID), dyspareunia |
| **Ovarian cysts** | Endometriomas or other cysts can cause cyclic pain and pressure | Pelvic fullness, bloating |
| **Cervical stenosis** | Narrowed cervical os obstructs menstrual outflow, increasing intrauterine pressure | Often very heavy cramping with scanty flow |

Endometriosis deserves particular mention because it is systematically underdiagnosed. The average delay from symptom onset to confirmed diagnosis of endometriosis is reported to be 6–10 years in many healthcare settings [2]. This delay is partly because symptoms are frequently attributed to "normal" period pain. If your dysmenorrhoea is worsening cycle by cycle, is associated with pain during intercourse, or is accompanied by difficulty conceiving, endometriosis should be actively investigated.

## How Dysmenorrhoea Is Diagnosed

### History

A detailed menstrual history is the starting point. The questions I focus on are: When did the pain begin in relation to menarche? Is it getting worse over time? Does it occur only during menstruation or at other points in the cycle? Is there pain during intercourse or bowel movements? What medications have been tried, and how effective are they?

### Examination

A pelvic examination assesses for uterine size, tenderness, nodularity in the Pouch of Douglas (which can suggest endometriosis), and adnexal masses.

### Investigations

- **Pelvic ultrasound** — the first-line imaging investigation; identifies fibroids, ovarian cysts, adenomyosis (on transvaginal ultrasound), and hydrosalpinx
- **Swabs and infection screen** — to exclude PID when relevant
- **MRI pelvis** — more detailed assessment of adenomyosis or deep infiltrating endometriosis
- **Diagnostic laparoscopy** — the gold standard for confirming endometriosis; required when ultrasound is normal but symptoms are strongly suggestive [3]

It is important to understand that a normal ultrasound does not rule out endometriosis. Superficial peritoneal endometriosis is invisible on ultrasound, and even endometriomas can be missed on suboptimal imaging. A normal scan in the presence of progressive, cyclical pain does not mean nothing is wrong.

## Treatment and Management Options

Treatment is guided by the type of dysmenorrhoea, severity, the woman's reproductive plans, and the underlying condition (if secondary).

### Primary dysmenorrhoea: management

| Approach | Options | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **NSAIDs** | Ibuprofen, mefenamic acid, naproxen | First-line; start at onset of symptoms or just before; take with food |
| **Combined oral contraceptive pill** | Suppresses ovulation and reduces endometrial prostaglandin production | Effective in 70–80% of women with primary dysmenorrhoea [4] |
| **Hormonal IUS (Mirena)** | Reduces endometrial shedding | Useful when contraception is also desired |
| **Heat therapy** | Local heat application (hot water bottle) | Evidence supports benefit as an adjunct |
| **Lifestyle** | Regular aerobic exercise, reduced caffeine, adequate sleep | Modest but real benefit; safe to encourage |
| **Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS)** | High-frequency TENS | Modest evidence; can be used alongside pharmacotherapy |

### Secondary dysmenorrhoea: management

Treatment of secondary dysmenorrhoea is directed at the underlying cause. A woman with endometriosis, fibroids, or adenomyosis requires a management plan specific to her diagnosis, her symptom burden, and whether she wishes to preserve fertility. This goes well beyond simple pain relief and warrants a dedicated consultation.

In my practice at Cloudnine Hospital, Sarjapur Road, Bengaluru, and at Docube Clinic, Doddakannelli, Bengaluru, I see a consistent pattern: women who have been managing progressively worsening dysmenorrhoea with higher and higher doses of over-the-counter painkillers for years, without ever having had a pelvic ultrasound or a proper gynaecological assessment. By the time they present, some have already developed complications from the underlying condition — whether that is an endometrioma requiring surgery, or adenomyosis causing significant anaemia from heavy bleeding. The message I want to give clearly is this: escalating pain relief year on year is not a diagnosis. It is a reason to investigate.

## When Dysmenorrhoea Is Not Normal — Red Flags

Primary dysmenorrhoea is common, but it should never be severe enough to consistently stop a woman from functioning, and it should not worsen over time. The following features suggest secondary dysmenorrhoea and should prompt gynaecological assessment:

- **Pain that worsens progressively** with each menstrual cycle
- **Pain that begins several days before** menstruation starts
- **Pelvic pain outside of menstruation** — mid-cycle, on defaecation, or during intercourse
- **Pain not controlled** by standard doses of over-the-counter NSAIDs
- **Associated heavy menstrual bleeding** or passage of large clots
- **Difficulty conceiving** after 12 months of unprotected intercourse (or 6 months if over 35)
- **Pelvic pain that begins for the first time after age 25**, without a prior history of dysmenorrhoea (this pattern is more consistent with secondary causes)

Over the years managing this condition, the single most common misconception I encounter is the belief that "my mother had the same pain, so it's just how it is in our family." A family history of painful periods does not mean the pain is untreatable — nor does it rule out endometriosis, which itself has a heritable component. Every woman's pain deserves proper evaluation.

## Dysmenorrhoea and Conditions That Share Features

Dysmenorrhoea frequently overlaps with, or can be a symptom of, other conditions that affect menstrual and reproductive health. Women with [polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)](/blog/pcos-symptoms-diagnosis-and-treatment-explained) can also experience irregular, painful periods, and the two conditions occasionally co-exist. Understanding the broader hormonal picture is important when assessing any woman with a complex menstrual history.

Endometriosis in particular requires its own detailed discussion, as its management spans hormonal therapy, surgical intervention, and long-term monitoring — far beyond what can be addressed within a general article on dysmenorrhoea. If you have been told you may have endometriosis, or if investigations are being planned, a dedicated consultation is important to map out the right approach for your individual situation.

## The Importance of Not Normalising Period Pain

The Federation of Obstetric and Gynaecological Societies of India (FOGSI) and gynaecological societies globally have consistently emphasised that severe dysmenorrhoea should not be normalised [5]. Cultural narratives that frame debilitating period pain as something women simply have to endure delay diagnosis and treatment, sometimes by years.

Across my gynaecological practice in Bengaluru, I regularly hear from patients who describe their pain as "just period pain" even when it has been preventing them from going to work or attending college for two or three days every month. Two or three days per month amounts to 24–36 days per year — the equivalent of up to five working weeks. That is not trivial, and it is not inevitable.

If you or a family member has been tolerating severe period pain without seeking evaluation, the right next step is a gynaecological assessment. A thorough history, a pelvic examination, and in many cases a single pelvic ultrasound can determine whether the pain is primary or secondary — and from there, an evidence-based plan can be made.

## Key Points

- Dysmenorrhoea is classified as primary (no underlying cause) or secondary (caused by a pelvic condition such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, or fibroids).
- Primary dysmenorrhoea is driven by prostaglandins, typically begins close to menarche, and does not worsen progressively over years.
- Secondary dysmenorrhoea tends to worsen cycle by cycle and is often accompanied by pain outside menstruation, heavy bleeding, or difficulty conceiving.
- A normal pelvic ultrasound does not rule out endometriosis; laparoscopy remains the gold-standard diagnostic investigation when endometriosis is clinically suspected.
- NSAIDs are first-line treatment for primary dysmenorrhoea and work best when started at or just before the onset of bleeding.
- Severe or worsening period pain that requires regular high-dose analgesics, affects daily functioning, or is accompanied by any red-flag features warrants prompt gynaecological evaluation — it should not be normalised.
